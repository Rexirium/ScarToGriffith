\section{Model and the Griffiths region}
We consider spins $s_i=\pm1$ on an $L\times L$ square lattice with periodic boundary conditions and
$N=L^2$ sites. Setting $k_{\mathrm B}=1$, the zero-field Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
H=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}s_i s_j,\qquad J_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{with probability }p,\\0,&\text{with probability }1-p.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Bonds are drawn independently and remain fixed during thermal sampling. This is the bond-diluted
limit of the ferromagnetic random-bond Ising model. Dataset \texttt{run\_005} uses $p=0.6$. We
denote thermal averages in realization $a$ by $\langle\cdot\rangle_a$ and disorder averages by
$[\cdot]_{\rm dis}$.

Disorder allows unusually well-connected, nearly pure regions inside a globally paramagnetic system.
In the Griffiths region, $T_c(p)<T<T_G$, these rare regions can generate enhanced responses and slow
relaxation, while the equilibrium free energy is nonanalytic in magnetic field at zero field. Here
$T_G=2/\ln(1+\sqrt2)\simeq2.269$ is the pure square-lattice transition temperature. Classical
Griffiths singularities can be weak: slow dynamics or broad response distributions alone do not
establish their asymptotic form.

The present concentration lies above the square-lattice bond-percolation threshold $p_c=1/2$.
A percolating occupied-bond network and a nonzero thermodynamic ordering temperature $T_c(0.6)$
are therefore possible and, for this model, expected. The scan covers both low-temperature ordering
behavior and the paramagnetic regime. We do not determine $T_c(0.6)$ from a single heat-capacity
maximum or classify every temperature below $T_G$ as part of the Griffiths region.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{phase_diagram.png}
\caption{Schematic temperature--bond-concentration phase diagram. The green curve denotes $T_c(p)$,
separating ferromagnetic order from the paramagnetic phase. The red line is the pure-system
transition temperature, labeled $T_f$ in the image and denoted $T_G$ here. The lightly shaded region
between these boundaries is the Griffiths region. The label $p_{\rm th.}$ corresponds to the
percolation threshold $p_c=1/2$. Black markers illustrate a scan at the present concentration
$p=0.6$; their temperatures are schematic rather than the complete simulation grid.}\label{fig:phase}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:phase} emphasizes that the Griffiths region is globally paramagnetic despite its
slowly relaxing, locally correlated rare regions. At fixed $p=0.6$, cooling crosses $T_G$ before
reaching $T_c(0.6)$. Below the latter temperature, bulk order also contributes to large responses
and persistent memory. The green curve is schematic and is not a fit to this dataset; a quantitative
phase assignment requires an independent finite-size analysis of the ordering transition.

\section{Monte Carlo procedure}
The simulations used SpinMonteCarlo.jl 1.2.2 with Julia 1.13.0. Each realization was thermalized
with $2048$ Swendsen--Wang (SW) updates: parallel neighboring spins are linked with probability
$1-e^{-2J_{ij}/T}$, and each resulting cluster is independently assigned either orientation.
Static observables were then measured after each of $16384$ additional SW updates. Local responses
used the same SW thermalization and measurement procedure. For the first disorder realization,
the local-response calculation reused its MC seed and reproduced the static measurement trajectory.

Dynamical measurements were performed separately. After the last SW measurement, its final spin
configuration was saved as the reference state and evolved for $4096$ random-site heat-bath sweeps.
Each sweep selects $N$ sites with replacement and resamples them according to
\begin{equation}
P(s_i=+1\mid\{s_j\})=\frac{1+\tanh(h_i/T)}{2},\qquad h_i=\sum_{j:\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}s_j.
\end{equation}
These heat-bath sweeps contribute only to the dynamical overlap, not to static averages. This
separation permits efficient equilibrium sampling while retaining a specified local stochastic
dynamics; SW update counts are not interpreted as physical relaxation times.

The sizes are $L=10,16,24,32,40,50$, with $T=0.02,0.04,\ldots,3.00$ and $1000$ disorder realizations
per size, giving $150$ temperatures. The same bond realizations are reused across temperatures,
with temperature-dependent MC seeds. The master seed is $1234$. Static measurements are divided
into $64$ blocks of $256$ SW updates for block statistics and jackknife estimates. Disorder-average
bands use the sample standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{1000}$; this scatter already includes
MC noise, so stored thermal errors are not added again. SW sampling improves cluster reorientation,
but convergence still requires checks of run length, block size, and independent seeds.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Disorder-averaged heat capacity and susceptibility}
For $M=\sum_i s_i$, the observables are
\begin{equation}
C_a=\frac{\langle H^2\rangle_a-\langle H\rangle_a^2}{T^2},\qquad \chi_a=\frac{\langle M^2\rangle_a}{T}.
\end{equation}
Both are total-system quantities; division by $N$ gives per-site values. The susceptibility uses the
exact symmetry $\langle M\rangle_a=0$ of the finite-volume zero-field ensemble, without subtracting
$\langle|M|\rangle_a^2$.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{01_mean_observables.png}
\caption{Disorder-averaged total heat capacity (left) and total susceptibility (right) for six
sizes in \texttt{run\_005}, with $p=0.6$. Shading denotes $\pm1$ standard error of the disorder mean
(SEM). The susceptibility axis is logarithmic.}\label{fig:mean}
\end{figure}
The heat-capacity maximum shifts systematically toward lower temperatures as $L$ increases. Its
sampled location is $T=1.24,1.16,1.10,1.08,1.06,1.04$ for the six sizes, respectively. For $L=50$,
$[C]_{\rm dis}/N\simeq0.586$ at the maximum. The per-site peak varies only moderately, from
$0.578$ at $L=10$ through $0.597$ at $L=24$ to $0.586$ at $L=50$. Thus much of the growth of the
total peak reflects volume, whereas the peak displacement and sharpening contain finite-size
information. Neither the peak height nor its location alone provides a thermodynamic transition
temperature. In particular, the selected plotting temperature $T=1.02$ is close to, but not equal
to, the $L=50$ heat-capacity maximum.

The susceptibility rises strongly upon cooling, without an isolated maximum in the displayed range.
For this symmetric-ensemble definition, a large low-temperature value can include the squared
magnetization of a macroscopically correlated cluster. It should not be interpreted solely as the
connected response within one selected ordered phase. At high temperature, the ordinary extensive
limit is $\chi\sim N/T$; separation between total-response curves alone does not demonstrate
anomalous scaling.

For a fixed finite bond realization, the low-temperature equilibrium limit is particularly useful:
$T\chi\to\sum_c n_c^2$, where $n_c$ is the size of each connected occupied-bond cluster and its
orientation is independently sampled. Reconstructing the $1000$ bond realizations at $L=50$ gives
$[\sum_c n_c^2]_{\rm dis}=5.62517\times10^6$. At $T=0.02$, the measured $[T\chi]_{\rm dis}$ agrees
with this value to better than $0.01\%$. The samplewise ratios $T\chi_a/\sum_c n_{c,a}^2$ have
5th and 95th percentiles $0.999775$ and $1.000188$. This supports the low-temperature SW sampling,
without establishing convergence at every temperature.

\subsection{Spatial structure of the local susceptibility}
The response of an individual spin to a uniform field is
\begin{equation}
\chi_{i,a}=\frac{\langle s_i M\rangle_a}{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_j\langle s_i s_j\rangle_a,\qquad \sum_i\chi_{i,a}=\chi_a.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:local} follows the first $L=50$ disorder realization across four temperatures, so
the bond geometry remains fixed.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.76\linewidth]{02_local_susceptibility.png}
\caption{SW estimates of local susceptibility at $T=0.30,0.70,1.02,2.00$ for the same bond
realization at $p=0.6$. Each panel uses an independent symmetric linear color scale. Negative
estimates are retained; equal color intensity across panels does not mean equal response.}\label{fig:local}
\end{figure}
At $T=0.30$, most of the map has a large, nearly uniform positive response, interspersed with small
weak-response regions. This is consistent with a dominant connected cluster: in the rigid-cluster
limit, a site in a cluster of size $n_c$ has $\chi_i=n_c/T$. At $T=0.70$, variations within the
strong-response region become visible, and at $T=1.02$ the response forms a more heterogeneous
pattern. By $T=2.00$, the overall scale is much smaller. The maximum estimated response falls from
approximately $7845$ at $T=0.30$ to $3119$, $705$, and $7.17$ in the remaining panels. These maps
show connectivity-dependent response, but the low-temperature large cluster is not by itself a
rare region inside a paramagnetic background.

Ferromagnetic equilibrium correlations imply $\chi_{i,a}\geq1/T$, whereas a finite-sample estimator
can be negative. In the four panels, $2.40\%$, $2.36\%$, $2.16\%$, and $0.60\%$ of sites have
negative estimates, respectively; none lies below minus three times its stored block SEM. A weakly
coupled or isolated spin can have a noisy $s_iM$ estimator because the instantaneous total
magnetization is large, even when its own mean response is small. The small negative estimates
therefore do not establish negative equilibrium response or antiferromagnetic patches. Their
reported errors still require block-length checks, and sitewise tests are spatially correlated.
The summed local estimates agree with the first sample's total susceptibility at all selected
sizes and temperatures, as expected from the shared SW trajectory.

\subsection{Sample-to-sample susceptibility distributions}
Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows kernel density estimates of $x=\log_{10}\chi$ from $1000$ realizations
at each size and temperature. Their normalization is
\begin{equation}
\int p_x(x)\,\mathrm dx=1,\qquad p_\chi(\chi)=\frac{p_x(\log_{10}\chi)}{\chi\ln10}.
\end{equation}
The ordinate is a density per unit logarithmic susceptibility, not per unit $\chi$.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.73\linewidth]{03_susceptibility_distributions.png}
\caption{Distributions of total susceptibility at six temperatures for $L=10,24,40,50$, normalized
in $\mathrm d(\log_{10}\chi)$. Smooth curves are kernel density estimates; narrow peaks have larger
density because their areas remain unity. Small shoulders require bandwidth and sampling checks
before interpretation.}\label{fig:dist}
\end{figure}
Cooling shifts the distributions toward larger susceptibility, consistent with Fig.~\ref{fig:mean}.
For the larger sizes, the low-temperature curves at $T=0.30,0.50,0.70$ are sharply concentrated,
whereas $T=1.02$ produces the broadest distribution among the six plotted temperatures. At $L=50$,
the standard deviations of $\log_{10}\chi$ are $0.0085,0.0096,0.0159,0.1674,0.0398,0.0177$ in
increasing temperature order. The broad intermediate-temperature response is compatible with
sample-dependent thermal disruption of correlated clusters near the ordering crossover; it does
not by itself locate $T_c$ or establish a Griffiths tail.

The relative width $\operatorname{std}(\chi)/[\chi]_{\rm dis}$ at $T=0.30$ decreases from $0.081$
for $L=10$ to $0.019$ for $L=50$. At low temperature and $p=0.6$, a dominant occupied cluster
can give a large response with relatively small sample-to-sample fluctuations. At $T=1.02$, the
relative width instead increases from $0.281$ to $0.369$ over these sizes. This fixed-temperature
trend motivates a finite-size study but is not an asymptotic self-averaging test at a known critical
point. At $T=1.50$ and $2.00$, the distributions narrow with increasing size.

The rightward displacement with size partly reflects the total-system normalization. Replacing
$\chi$ by $\chi/N$ translates a logarithmic distribution by $-\log_{10}N$ without changing its
width. The densities include both quenched disorder variation and residual MC noise; independent
SW runs and block-size checks remain necessary for quantitative tail analysis. No power-law fit,
Griffiths exponent, or universal distribution is inferred from these kernel density estimates.

\subsection{Autocorrelation under heat-bath dynamics}
The reference state is the final SW configuration after thermalization and static measurement.
Starting from this state, the measured heat-bath overlap is
\begin{equation}
A_a(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i s_i(t_0+t)s_i(t_0),\qquad 0\leq t\leq4096.
\end{equation}
We use $A(t)$ to distinguish it from heat capacity; the plot labels it $C(t)$. Here $t$ counts
heat-bath sweeps from the reference, not SW updates. Each sample contributes one fixed-origin
trajectory, without time-origin averaging or subtraction of spin means.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.79\linewidth]{04_autocorrelation.png}
\caption{Disorder-averaged overlap with $\pm1$ SEM. One time unit is one random-site heat-bath
sweep following the last SW measurement. The horizontal axis is logarithmic and the vertical axis
is linear. The stored value $A(0)=1$ is omitted from the logarithmic time axis; nonpositive
estimates and full SEM bands are retained.}\label{fig:corr}
\end{figure}
The low-temperature curves show a rapid initial drop followed by persistent memory. At $L=50$,
$[A(4096)]_{\rm dis}\simeq0.955,0.920,0.834$ for $T=0.30,0.50,0.70$, respectively, with
relatively modest size dependence. Weakly constrained spins can relax quickly while large,
strongly correlated regions retain their orientation. At $p>p_c$, such behavior can include
low-temperature ferromagnetic correlations and must not automatically be assigned to the
paramagnetic Griffiths region.

At $T=1.02$, the $L=50$ overlap decreases from $0.533$ at $128$ sweeps to $0.325\pm0.004$ at
$4096$ sweeps, resolving substantial relaxation beyond the initial window. The final value varies
nonmonotonically with size, from $0.284$ at $L=10$ to $0.372$ at $L=24$ and $0.325$ at $L=50$.
At $T=1.50$ and $2.00$, the $L=50$ endpoint values are $-0.00047\pm0.00139$ and
$0.00022\pm0.00095$, respectively, consistent with zero within the reported SEM.

These curves characterize local heat-bath dynamics, not the autocorrelation time of SW sampling.
For a finite system at positive temperature, sufficiently long heat-bath evolution can ultimately
reverse the magnetization; a finite-window plateau is not proof of a nonzero infinite-time limit.
The logarithmic time axis exposes early and late relaxation, but a straight segment on this
semi-logarithmic plot is not evidence for a power law. Longer windows and independent reference
trajectories are needed to distinguish decay forms. The SEM combines disorder variation with
single-trajectory noise, and adjacent times remain correlated.

\section{Summary}
The four numerical figures describe static responses and local stochastic relaxation of the
$p=0.6$ bond-diluted Ising model. Above the occupied-bond percolation threshold, the low-temperature
regime can support ferromagnetic order, whereas the Griffiths region is the paramagnetic interval
between $T_c(0.6)$ and the pure-system temperature $T_G$. This distinction is necessary when
interpreting both large susceptibility and long-lived memory.

The heat-capacity maximum moves from $T=1.24$ to $1.04$ as $L$ grows from $10$ to $50$, while
its per-site height changes comparatively little. This finite-size drift deserves further analysis,
but the heat-capacity maximum alone does not determine the ordering temperature. The measured
susceptibility uses the symmetric finite-volume ensemble and grows strongly upon cooling, with
large connected clusters contributing through their squared magnetization.

SW updates are used for both thermalization and static measurements. A low-temperature check
against the exact occupied-cluster expression $T\chi\to\sum_c n_c^2$ supports the $L=50$
magnetic-response estimates. The local maps show a largely coherent low-temperature response that
becomes spatially heterogeneous upon heating. Small negative local estimates remain possible
because $s_iM$ fluctuates strongly; the displayed maps do not show large negative responses beyond
three reported block standard errors. This is useful evidence about sampling quality, not a
replacement for convergence tests across temperatures and observables.

The susceptibility distributions add information beyond their means. Low-temperature response is
large but relatively narrowly distributed for the larger sizes, while the selected temperature
$T=1.02$ exhibits substantial sample-to-sample variation. At higher temperatures, the distributions
narrow with size. The response widths are consistent with changing connectivity and thermal
correlation scales, but do not establish a universal tail, a lack of self-averaging, or a Griffiths
exponent.

The subsequent heat-bath trajectories retain strong low-temperature memory over $4096$ sweeps,
show a slower decay near $T=1.02$, and approach zero at $T=1.50$ and $2.00$. Efficient SW
sampling and slow local dynamics are compatible: they use different transition rules. Neither a
finite-window plateau nor spatial heterogeneity alone identifies the rare-region contribution in
the paramagnetic phase.

For quantitative conclusions, the next steps are to determine $T_c(0.6)$ through Binder crossings
and finite-size consistency checks, verify static errors with longer runs and different block
sizes, and extend dynamical sampling with independent reference states and longer observation
windows. Separating equilibrium sampling, the specified heat-bath dynamics, and the location of
the ordering transition is essential for a controlled investigation of Griffiths behavior.
